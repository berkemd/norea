The pieces that make the most outfits

A short, practical guide to getting more outfits out of fewer clothes. From the makers of Closetwise.

Outfits multiply; pieces only add

An outfit is roughly one top, one bottom and one pair of shoes. If every piece goes with every other piece, the number of outfits is the product of the three counts, not their sum. Five tops, four bottoms and three pairs of shoes are twelve pieces, and up to 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 outfits. Add one more bottom that goes with all five tops and all three pairs of shoes, and you gain fifteen outfits from a single purchase.

The catch is the word if. A bright patterned shirt that only works with one pair of trousers adds three outfits at most (one per pair of shoes), however much you like it. So the useful question before buying is not "is this nice?" but "how many of my pieces does this go with?"

Start from a neutral base

Neutral colours (navy, grey, black, white, cream, camel, olive, denim blue) go with almost everything, including each other. That is why they multiply. A practical base for most people:

  • Two or three plain T-shirts or knit tops in white, grey or navy: they sit under a jacket, a cardigan or on their own.
  • One pair of dark jeans and one pair of trousers in a colour you can dress up and down (navy, grey or black).
  • One layer: a blazer, an overshirt or a cardigan that turns a casual outfit into a work one.
  • One pair of plain trainers and one pair of smarter shoes.

Colour and pattern belong in the pieces you change most easily: a shirt, a scarf, a bag. One strong piece on a neutral base reads as a choice; three strong pieces together usually fight.

The piece people most often need: a good plain T-shirt

When Closetwise ranks pieces by how many new outfits they would make with what someone already owns, a plain, well-cut T-shirt is often near the top of the list. It works under a blazer for the office, with chinos for a weekend and with dark trousers in the evening, and it is the first thing to wear out. Look for a weight that is not see-through, a neckline that keeps its shape after washing, and a fit that sits close at the shoulder. Three checks before you buy

  1. It goes with at least three things you already own. Picture the outfits now, in the shop, not "I'll find something".
  2. It suits the dress code you need most. If you are in an office four days a week, office-ready pieces earn their place first.
  3. You would wear it in more than one season. A light knit, a mid-weight jacket or straight-leg trousers work for most of the year; a heavy coat does one job.

Read cost per wear, not price

Price divided by the number of times you wear something is a fairer measure than the price tag. A €60 pair of shoes worn twice a week for a year is under 60 cents a wear; a €25 top worn twice is €12.50 a wear. Spend more on the things you will wear constantly (shoes, the everyday jacket, the trousers you live in) and less on pieces you wear for one occasion.

Keep what you have in rotation

  • See everything. Pieces you cannot see do not get worn. Fold or hang by kind and colour so the whole range is visible.
  • Notice what you never wear. If something has not been worn in a season, ask why: fit, comfort or nothing to wear it with. The last one is often fixed by one neutral piece.
  • Plan around the week, not the morning. Deciding a few outfits ahead, with the weather and your plans in mind, removes the daily guesswork and repeats less.
