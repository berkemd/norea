Scrim

Every camera app draws you a histogram and a level. Both look certain, and both are quietly wrong. Scrim measures the same two things and reports what it actually knows — including the places where the answer is that it cannot know.

What it does differently

The histogram is of the preview, not of the scene

By the time a frame reaches the preview it has been tone-mapped and quantised to eight bits. A saturated pixel may be one stop over the ceiling or five, and nothing in that preview distinguishes the two. Scrim shows the clipping — which is observed — and refuses to invent the amount, which is not. When the top end is saturated the range stops being a number and becomes a floor: at least 9.4 stops, written with a “≥”, never as a point.

The band is split by where it comes from

Two sources, kept apart because they behave differently. Quantisation: preview codes are whole numbers, and near black one code step is worth more than a quarter of a stop — that error does not shrink with more pixels, so it adds linearly. Sampling: the percentile is read from a finite number of pixels and wanders a little each frame — that adds in quadrature. Merging them into one comforting number would hide which one you could actually do something about.

Below code 5 a single step costs more than a quarter of a stop, so that is where Scrim stops calling the shadows measured. The number is not a preference; it is where the rule lands.

The level prints tenths of a degree it does not have

Roll comes from the horizontal component of the gravity vector, and that component goes to zero as the phone points up or down. The same accelerometer noise therefore means something different in every orientation: the band grows as 1/h. Held upright it is ±0.12°. At 80° of pitch it is ±0.70°. At 88° it is ±3.48° — wider than any tilt the screen could show, which is another way of saying the horizon is not defined there. Past that point Scrim stops printing an angle.

There is a third answer, and you will see it often

Between “level” and “tilted” sits undecided: the band straddles the tolerance and neither claim is available. Most camera apps have no way to say this, so they say the other thing instead. Scrim treats it as an answer, not a failure — and on the frame decision the same rule applies: shoot, fix this first, or I can’t measure this.

Converging verticals, with the assumption in view

How much your verticals converge is computed from pitch and your lens. The lens has no default: without a field of view the number cannot exist at all, so Scrim asks rather than guessing. The flat-façade assumption is printed next to the result, because the number is a model output and not a measurement of the building.

The delivery gap

A gain-map photo carries highlights above SDR white. How many of them a viewer sees depends on their screen, not yours — and every HDR indicator in the category measures yours. Scrim shows what an SDR screen, a typical phone and a bright HDR display each keep of a 2.4-stop file, and what each compresses away.

On the wrist, one decision

Phone on a tripod, you in the frame: the watch carries the decision and its band, and nothing else. No histogram, no shutter, no settings. What crosses to the watch is numbers — the decision, the angle, the band, the clipped fraction — never an image.

One purchase. No subscription and no in-app purchases. iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch. Six languages of interface.

What it will not do

  • It will not print a number in place of a range, or a range in place of “I can’t measure this”.
  • It will not guess how far past the ceiling a clipped highlight went.
  • It will not keep a frame. Preview buffers become a histogram and are released.
  • It will not make a network request, ask for your photo library, your location or your microphone, or create an account. Camera is the only permission it asks for.

The privacy lines are gates, not promises

Each claim above corresponds to a check that runs on every build and fails it if the claim stops being true: the source is scanned with comments stripped for URLSession, URLRequest, CFNetwork, NWConnection, CLLocationManager and StoreKit, and for anything that could write an image to disk. The permission strings that are supposed to be absent are checked for absence, and the one that is supposed to be present is checked for presence. A promise nobody measures is worse than no promise, because people rely on it.

How the numbers here were produced

Every figure on this page and in the App Store screenshots comes from the shipping core, run over scenes built from a written rule — a sum of Gaussian lobes in code space, three million pixels — not from a design file. The core carries 58 tests, and a mutation gate breaks each of its 50 defences one at a time to check that a test actually goes red. Three defences were found to be unreachable that way and were deleted rather than left in place looking measured.
