STAR method examples

A free guide from Eidos · from a written work example to a two-minute interview answer

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Interviewers use it because it forces an answer to stay on one real event. If you keep a work log or a brag document, you already have the material. This page shows how one written example turns into a spoken answer.

Four worked answers

These are illustrations. Use your own event, your own part in it and a result you may share.

"Tell me about a problem you solved."

Situation: In spring, support tickets about failed exports doubled within a month.

Task: I owned the export feature, so finding the cause was mine.

Action: I grouped the tickets by file type and saw that almost all involved one format. I reproduced the failure, fixed the converter and added a check that warns the user before the export starts.

Result: Tickets were back to their usual level the following month, and the check has caught the same case several times since.

"Describe a time you disagreed with a colleague."

Situation: A colleague and I disagreed about delaying a launch to fix a slow page.

Task: We had to give our manager one recommendation by Friday.

Action: I suggested we each write down what would have to be true for the other option to be right. That showed we disagreed about one number, so I measured it with real traffic over two days.

Result: The measurement supported launching on time with the fix a week later. We recommended that together, and it is what happened.

"Tell me about a mistake."

Situation: I accepted two deadlines in the same month.

Task: Both projects were mine to deliver.

Action: When I saw the second would slip, I told the client a week ahead and proposed a new date. Afterwards I agreed with my manager that I confirm capacity before accepting a second deadline.

Result: The project arrived a week late with the client informed. Nothing has slipped since.

"Give an example of leading without authority."

Situation: After an outage, three teams each had a different account of what went wrong.

Task: Nobody owned the review, so I offered to run it.

Action: I collected a timeline from each team, ran one meeting to merge them and wrote four follow-up actions, each with an owner and a date.

Result: All four were finished within two months, and the same failure has not happened again.

Common mistakes

  • All situation, no action. If the background takes a minute, the interviewer learns about the company, not about you.
  • "We" all the way through. Give the team's result, then say "my part was".
  • No result. End on what changed. If you never found out, say what you would check.
  • A general habit instead of one event. "I usually" is not STAR. "Last March" is.
  • Confidential detail. Describe the kind of client and the kind of number. An interviewer notices how you treat a former employer's information.

Practise from your own examples

  1. List the projects you would talk about, one line each.
  2. For each, write the situation, your contribution and the outcome in the brag document structure.
  3. Say each one aloud once with a timer. Cut the situation until the action takes more than half.
  4. Note which common questions each example can answer.

Common questions

How long should a STAR answer be?

About ninety seconds to two minutes when spoken. Spend most of that on the action. Two sentences are enough for the situation and the task together.

How many examples should I prepare?

Six to eight covers most interviews: a result you are proud of, a problem you solved, a conflict, a mistake, a time you led, a time you learned something quickly, and one or two that fit the role closely. One example can answer several questions if you change the emphasis.

What if the result was not good?

Say so, then give what you changed afterwards. Interviewers ask about failures to hear whether you can describe one plainly, and an answer that turns every failure into a hidden success sounds rehearsed.
