Self-review examples

A free guide from Eidos · sample self-assessment sentences, each with the evidence a reviewer will ask for

Most self-review advice gives you polished sentences to copy. The trouble is that a reviewer cannot tell a copied sentence from a true one. What makes a self-review convincing is the example behind each claim. Below, every sample sentence comes in two versions, vague and specific, with a note on the evidence worth keeping so you can answer the follow-up question.

The examples are illustrations. Swap in your own project, your own part in it and a result you are allowed to share.

The pattern behind every strong sentence

Claim — the skill or result, in plain words.

My contribution — what you did, separate from the team.

Outcome — what changed, with a number or a before-and-after.

Source — a document, ticket or message you may point to.

Delivering results

Vague: I consistently delivered high-quality work on time.

Specific: I led the billing migration from planning to launch. We moved every account in the planned eight weeks, and I wrote the rollback plan we ended up not needing.

Evidence to keep: the project plan with dates, the launch note, the rollback document.

Vague: I handled a heavy workload well this year.

Specific: I closed three customer projects in the second half of the year while covering a colleague's accounts for six weeks.

Evidence to keep: the project list with close dates, the handover note.

Vague: I always meet my targets.

Specific: I reached my quarterly target in three of four quarters. In the quarter I missed, two renewals slipped into the next month, and I have since moved renewal calls four weeks earlier.

Evidence to keep: the quarterly summary, the revised renewal calendar.

Solving problems

Vague: I am a strong problem solver.

Specific: Support tickets about failed exports doubled in spring. I traced them to one file format, fixed the converter and added a check, and the tickets returned to their usual level the following month.

Evidence to keep: the ticket trend, the fix, the date it shipped.

Vague: I improved our processes.

Specific: Month-end reporting took two people two days. I rebuilt the template so the figures fill in automatically, and one person now finishes it in a morning.

Evidence to keep: the old and new template, a note from the person who runs it now.

Working with others

Vague: I am a great team player.

Specific: When the design team was short-staffed in the autumn, I took over usability sessions for their two launches so both shipped on schedule.

Evidence to keep: the session notes, the thank-you message from the design lead, with permission.

Vague: I communicate well with stakeholders.

Specific: I started a short written update every Friday for the sales team. The weekly status meeting was cancelled after a month because nobody needed it.

Evidence to keep: three sample updates, the calendar change.

Leading and mentoring

Vague: I showed leadership.

Specific: I ran the incident review after the June outage, agreed four follow-up actions with three teams and checked that all four were finished by August.

Evidence to keep: the review document, the action list with completion dates.

Vague: I mentored junior colleagues.

Specific: I onboarded two new analysts. Both ran their first client report on their own within six weeks, using the checklist I wrote for them.

Evidence to keep: the onboarding checklist, the dates of their first reports.

Learning and growth

Vague: I am always learning.

Specific: I completed the cloud certification in March and used it in May to move our test environment, which cut its monthly cost.

Evidence to keep: the certificate, the cost comparison you are allowed to share.

Vague: I took on new responsibilities.

Specific: I became the first point of contact for our largest supplier in April and renegotiated the delivery schedule in September.

Evidence to keep: the announcement, the new schedule.

Areas for improvement

Reviewers read this section more closely than any other. Name one real thing, say what you already changed, and say how anyone could tell.

Vague: I sometimes take on too much.

Specific: I agreed to two projects in the same month and delivered one of them a week late. I now confirm capacity with my manager before accepting a second deadline, and nothing has slipped since July.

Evidence to keep: the two project dates, the note where the new rule was agreed.

Vague: I could improve my communication.

Specific: My handover notes were too short for people outside the team. I now use a fixed five-line format, and the last two handovers needed no follow-up questions.

Evidence to keep: one old and one new handover note.

Before you submit

  1. Check that every claim has an example and every example has a date.
  2. Rewrite each "we" into the team's result and your own part.
  3. Remove client names, personal data and anything confidential.
  4. Cut adjectives. "Fast", "strong" and "excellent" add nothing a date and a number do not.
  5. Keep the sources somewhere you can reach them during the conversation.

Common questions

How long should a self-review be?

Follow the form first. If it gives no limit, two to four short paragraphs per question is enough: one sentence for the claim, one or two for what you did, one for the result. A reviewer reading twenty of these will thank you for the short version.

What if I have no numbers?

Use a before-and-after instead. Describe what was true before your work and what is true now: a process that took two people now takes one, a question the team used to ask every week is answered in a document. A dated example beats an invented percentage.

How do I write about something that went badly?

State what happened in one sentence, what you changed afterwards, and where the change can be seen. Leave out the apology and the blame. A reviewer is looking for whether the same problem would happen again.

Should I say I or we?

Both, in separate sentences. Give the team's result with we, then your own part with I. Readers discount a self-review that claims a whole team's outcome, and they also cannot credit you for work hidden inside we.
