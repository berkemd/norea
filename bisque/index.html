Bisque

Bisque opens on a plain white figure. Unglazed, unpainted, nobody yet. Your child paints it, and it wakes up.

The word is real: bisque is fired clay that has not been glazed — a piece waiting for colour. It is also, historically, the name for a porcelain doll.

What it does differently

Painting, not filling in

Almost everything called “3D colouring” is a menu: pick a region, pick a colour, the region changes. Bisque paints onto the surface itself. A crayon catches on the texture and darkens in the creases. Watercolour pools where the form curves and leaves a ring at the edge. Glaze catches the light when the figure turns. Your child will not know why it feels right. That is the point.

Your child’s colours decide who it is

Warm colours give a livelier creature; cool ones, a quieter one. The rule is simple on purpose, because a child has to be able to discover it. In this category the child’s choices are decoration — the outfit is a sticker, the character never changes. Here the choice reaches the result.

No words, anywhere

The creature does not speak. It makes sounds — delight, curiosity, sleepiness — the way a creature in a good animated film does. There is no reading in the app at all. That is not a shortcut: it is what makes the app work identically for a four-year-old and in every language, with no translation to get wrong.

New things, without downloading any

Every day brings a different sky, a different visiting creature, different brushes, a different little place. None of it is downloaded, bought or unlocked. It is generated on the device, seeded by the date and by your child’s own paintings, so two children never get the same day and it does not run out.

This is the honest answer to “constantly refreshed content”. In this category that phrase means a treadmill of downloadable packs, each one either a purchase or an advert — two things that should not be sold to a child. Generating instead of downloading costs more engineering and no money.

The hard part was the limit, not the variety

A generator that assembles parts at random produces a monster within the hour, and a monster is not an acceptable defect in a toy. So “cute” is not a style here; it is a written set of measurements. Proportions from the infant schema described by Konrad Lorenz. Eyes in the lower half of the face, large but not insectile. No spindly limbs — thin limbs read as spider. No muddy colours, no neon: one looks dirty on a child’s screen, the other is a warning colour.

The second number matters as much as the first: a rule set that rejects nothing would also produce a clean first line. The third and fourth exist because constraining is easy and constraining while leaving variety is not — a generator that made the same creature every time would pass the first test too.

What it will never do

No advertising. No in-app purchases. No loot boxes, no timers, no streaks, no stars and no levels — painting has no right answer, and scoring one says otherwise. No accounts, no sign-in, no chat, no sharing; nothing that can reach your child from outside.

For parents

Bisque makes no network request of any kind. Nothing about your child leaves the device, because nothing is collected in the first place. It asks for no permissions: no camera, no microphone, no contacts, no location, no notifications. Each of those sentences is a check that runs on every build and stops the release if it stops being true — see Privacy.

Apple’s Kids Category rules are the strictest set the App Store has. For most apps they are a constraint. For this one they describe where it already was.

Price

One purchase. No subscription — a toy is not rented. No in-app purchases of any kind.

Availability

iPhone and iPad, ages 5 and under and just as good above. English, Turkish, German, French, Spanish and Japanese — though the app itself contains no words to translate.

There is no Apple Watch version, and that is a decision rather than an omission: a painting surface does not fit on a wrist.
